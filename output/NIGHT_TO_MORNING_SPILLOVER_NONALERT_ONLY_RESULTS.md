# Is the H2 spillover effect detectable on counties that were NOT themselves alerted?

Source: `code/run_night_to_morning_spillover_nonalert_only.py` ->
`output/tables/reg_night_to_morning_spillover_nonalert_only.csv`

## The question

The H2 headline joint model regresses fatal crashes on `cross_spillover`
while linearly controlling for the recipient county's own `night_alert`
status, on the full national county-day panel:

```
pooled (night_alert==0 or 1), robust FE:  beta=+0.030464, se=0.011635, p=0.0117, n=7,348,614
```

This asks a sharper version of the driving-the-effect question: does a
detectable spillover effect survive when the sample is restricted to
county-days where the recipient county was **not itself under any alert**
that night -- i.e. a "clean" test of "a commuter drives in from an
alerted home county" without the receiving county having simultaneously
issued (or been geo-expanded into) an alert of its own?

## Why this matters: exposure is structurally compounded, not just correlated

`run_state_dot_analysis_fixed._expand_statewide_rows()` geo-expands each
statewide AMBER alert (~65% of all alerts) to every county in that state.
On a statewide-campaign night, most or all of that state's counties are
simultaneously flagged `night_alert=1` for themselves AND receive large
`cross_spillover` mass from their many other alerted in-state neighbors
via commuting -- the two "separate" regressors are not just correlated,
they are mechanically generated by the same event for the highest-dose
spillover observations:

```
Of 196,174 county-days with cross_spillover > 0:
  20,489 (10.4%) also have the recipient's own night_alert == 1
    -> mean cross_spillover = 0.2216
  175,685 (89.6%) have night_alert == 0 (recipient not itself alerted)
    -> mean cross_spillover = 0.0037
  (a ~61x gap in the exposure variable's own scale)
```

A linear `night_alert` control cannot fully separate "driving in from an
alerted county" from "living in a county that was itself swept into the
same statewide campaign" when nearly all of the variable's identifying
magnitude sits on days both are simultaneously true.

## Result

Restricting to `night_alert==0` (dropping only the 20,518 of 7,348,614
row, 0.28%, county-days where the recipient was itself alerted, but
losing 20,489 of the 196,174, 10.4%, nonzero-cross_spillover rows --
precisely the highest-dose ones):

```
night_alert==0 only, robust FE:              beta=+0.028387, se=0.038460, p=0.464, n=7,328,096
night_alert==0 only, naive FE:               beta=+0.029738, se=0.039452, p=0.455, n=7,328,096
night_alert==0 only, robust FE, serious inj: beta=+0.027443, se=0.022073, p=0.220, n=7,328,096
```

The point estimate barely moves (0.0284 vs. 0.0305 -- about 93% of the
pooled magnitude) but the standard error more than **triples** (0.0116 ->
0.0385) and the result becomes completely undetectable (p=0.46). Removing
just 0.28% of rows costs almost all of the statistical power because it
removes 10.4% of the nonzero-exposure observations -- specifically the
ones with ~60x the typical exposure magnitude.

## Bottom line

**There is no statistically detectable spillover effect on counties that
were not themselves under an active alert that night.** The pooled H2
estimate's apparent precision comes overwhelmingly from statewide
alert-campaign nights, when a work county both (a) receives high
commuting-weighted exposure from many simultaneously alerted in-state
neighbors and (b) is itself geo-expanded into "directly alerted" status
by the same statewide broadcast. The point estimate is directionally
consistent either way (positive, similar magnitude), so this is not
evidence the effect reverses -- but it means the current design cannot
distinguish "a commuter carries a sleep-disruption effect from home county
to work county" from "statewide alert-campaign nights are associated with
more crashes everywhere in the state, for some other reason entirely
(e.g., heightened public attention, weather, or another correlate of
statewide campaigns)," since almost all of the identifying variation comes
from days where both are true simultaneously. This is a materially
different and more specific caveat than the leave-one-state-out finding in
`NIGHT_TO_MORNING_LOO_RESULTS.md`: it is not about a handful of states
carrying the estimate, but about the compound nature of the exposure
variable itself.
